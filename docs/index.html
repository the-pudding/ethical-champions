This is a chart of every NBA champion since 2001, ranked by how ethical their title was.

Way out on the left are the 2015 Warriors, by far the least ethical champion of the century by my measure. They got lucky, facing multiple opponents missing important players.

But some champs faced a harder road. I believe there have only been five truly ethical NBA champions in the last 25 years: teams that don’t need an asterisk by their title.

Ethical NBA Championships, Ranked (big title)

To make the case, let’s start back in 2001. A simpler time, when AI just meant Haley Joel Osment playing a robot learning to be a real boy.

Back then, players weren’t often absent for the playoffs. Lately, though, the playoffs have become a war of attrition. Here is how many games above-average players (defined shortly) missed each year.

But let’s be real: a missing 40 year-old Karl Malone on his last legs is not the same as a prime Kevin Durant.

Plenty of all-in-one stats have been built to quantify a player’s impact. We’ll use DARKO. Without getting too in the weeds, it estimates how much a player helps their team win, measured in points added per 100 possessions versus a league-average player.

Most importantly, DARKO is a forward-looking metric that blends things like box-scores, play-by-play, and age to answer the question “how good is this player right now?” Or in our case, “how much impact is this team missing?”

Roughly speaking, +1 is a solid starter, +3 is a star, and +6 is MVP tier. 0 is a league-average player, replaceable.

With DARKO, we can quantify the impact that is missing from each playoffs by year, using the end-of-regular season DARKO scores (DPM). Now we can go from counting missing games to measuring the missing impact in each one.

I’ve chosen to limit to players of at least +1 DPM, which on average is about 125 players per year. Here is the average DPM missed per-game each year, counting every missing starter-level player.

Now let’s focus on NBA champions’ opponents. Charts are fine for general trends, but a pile of heads might help put the volume, and the humans behind it, in context. Here is every game missed by the 2015 Warriors’ opponents.

Jrue Holiday, Mike Conley, Tony Allen, Anderson Varejao, Kevin Love, and Kyrie Irving all missed games in three of the four rounds. Our champs got to walk through the playoffs avoiding these players, making the path undeniably easier. I’m not saying it was easy, but don’t tell me they didn’t get a little lucky not facing a full-strength Cavaliers roster.

This shows us the numerical advantage each champ got from missing opponents. But that’s only half of the equation. Because the champs might have had their own missing players. That makes things harder, and can balance things out.

People harp on the 2025 (and beyond) Thunder for their questionable bucket-getting approaches. They might not get ethical buckets, but they did get an ethical championship.

In 2025 the Thunder caught a couple breaks by dodging Ja Morant and Brandon Clarke on the Grizzlies.

But the Thunder had their own setbacks, missing Kenrich Williams for nearly the whole run, Aaron Wiggins for a game, and Isaiah Joe for some pivotal Finals games. While not superstar level players, they were still important pieces. Subtract what they were missing from what their opponents were missing, and it yields an, analytically speaking, ethical championship.

Of the five that are quantitatively ethical, three jump out that are worthy of the ethical crown.

First, the technical champs, the 2012 Miami Heat. They crushed the competition despite missing their third musketeer, Chris Bosh, for two of the four rounds.

But for me, the purest of the ethical champions had to be one of the teams that faced unblemished rosters, leaving no doubt in my mind that they outperformed the best competition. Between the 2004 Detroit Pistons and the 2006 Miami Heat, a slight nod towards the Heat for two reasons.

First, the Pistons technically didn’t have to face Karl Malone for one game. His DPM was below the threshold and he wasn’t exactly a key guy, he wasn’t nothing.

Second, the Heat missed a couple games in the first round without Alonzo Mourning. Also, they technically missed two games from below threshold, but still important players in James Posey and Udonis Haslem.
